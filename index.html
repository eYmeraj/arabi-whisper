Possessives

My, your, his: whose it is goes on the end of the word, as in albi, my heart.

The endings

After a vowel, the vowel gets long and takes the stress: kur-SEEK, your chair. For his, that is all there is: kur-SEE, his chair.

The same endings go on any word: 'omri, my life; albak, your heart; habeebi, my love.

Changes to know

Words ending in -a get a t back
shanta, bag
myshantitihershantit-ha
your m.shantitakourshantitna
your f.shantitikyour pl.shantitku
hisshantitutheirshantit-hum

Before -ha, -na, -ku and -hum it is always -it-. Before the other endings the i only comes after two consonants, as in shanta, or 'arabiyya, car: 'arabiyyiti, my car. After one consonant it doesn't: habeeba, love, habibti, my love (the ee gets short, as in the next rule).

Long vowels get short before two consonants

kitaab, book: kitaabi, kitaabak, kitaabu, but kitabha, kitabna, kitabhum. ei gets short too: beiti, my house, but betna, our house.

Two consonants at the end: a helping vowel

ukht, sister: ukhti, ukhtak, ukhtik, ukhtu, but ukhtaha, ukhtina, ukhtuku, ukhtuhum. The same with esm, name, so "what's her name?" is esmaha eih?

Father and brother add -oo-
abb, father
myabooyaherabooha
your m.abookouraboona
your f.abookiyour pl.abooku
hisabootheiraboohum

After the -oo- come the endings for a vowel. The same with akh, brother: akhooya, my brother; akhook, your brother. Most people just say baba and mama.

bitaa': belonging to

Instead of an ending, Egyptians often put bitaa' after the word, which then takes el-. bitaa' matches the word:

masculineel-mobayl bitaa'imy phone
feminineel-'arabiyya bita'timy car
pluralel-kutub bitoo'imy books

It takes the same endings: bitaa'ak, bita'ha, bita'na, bita'hum. It is the natural choice for words from other languages, and to stress whose it is. Family and the body always take the ending: ukhti, never el-ukht bita'ti.

Whose, with a name or a noun

Put the two words side by side, the thing first: beit Ahmad, Ahmad's house. Only the second word can take el-: beit el-walad, the boy's house. A word ending in -a gets its t back, as before an ending: 'arabiyyit abooya, my father's car.

The same endings on 'and, ma'a and verbs

They go on words like 'and (at) and ma'a (with): 'andi, I have (at me); ma'aaya, with me. On verbs and on 'aayiz, me is -ni: shuftini, you saw me; 'ayzaani, she wants me. So it is after min (from) and 'an (about): minni, 'anni.

'ayzak ma'aaya 'ala tool ya habeebi, I want you with me always, my love, has three: 'ayzak, you; ma'aaya, me; habeebi, my.

Verbs

Every verb has two stems: katab, he wrote, and yektib, he writes. The past adds an ending for who; the present adds a prefix, and for some an ending too.

The bare form is now without be-: aktib, tektib, yektibu. For ana, be- and ha- take over the a-: baktib, haktib. In fast speech the prefixes squeeze: beyektib sounds like byektib.

katabt is both I wrote and you wrote (to a man), and betektib both you write (to a man) and she writes. The pronoun tells them apart: enta katabt? did you write?

The present vowel: learn verbs in pairs

The vowel of the present stem can be i, a or u:

katab / yektibwrite
shirib / yeshrabdrink
'amal / ye'mildo, make

talab / yutluborder, ask for

Verbs like shirib drop their second i before an ending that starts with a vowel: shiribt, I drank, but shirbit, she drank, and shirbu, they drank.

Participles

Want, know, need. Egyptian has no everyday verb for want: it says 'aayiz, wanting. Like an adjective, it changes only for who wants: a man, a woman or a group. The examples are a man speaking to a man, unless they say otherwise.

'aayiz · want
Who wantsFormWith
a man'aayiz عايزana, enta, huwwa
a woman'ayza عايزةana, enti, hiyya
a group'ayzeen عايزينehna, entu, humma

So ana 'aayiz, enta 'aayiz and huwwa 'aayiz are I want, you want and he wants, and the pronoun usually stays. You'll also hear 'aawiz, 'awza, 'awzeen: the same word.

The verb after 'aayiz is always bare, and it is for whoever does it. If that is the one who wants, they match: ana 'aayiz arooh. If it is someone else, put them on 'aayiz as an ending, and the verb is theirs: 'ayzak terooh, I want you to go. Never barooh or harooh after 'aayiz.

More

Why the forms change

A man's 'aayiz drops its i before an ending that starts with a vowel: 'aayiz + ak, 'ayzak. Before one that starts with a consonant, the i stays and takes the stress, so the aa gets short: 'aayiz + ha, 'ayizha.

A woman's 'ayza stretches its last a to aa before any ending, and the stress moves onto it: 'ayzaak. That long aa is the only sign that a woman is speaking: AY-zak from a man, ay-ZAAK from a woman.

For a group, the ending goes on 'ayzeen: 'ayzeenak, we or they want you; 'ayzeenu, we or they want it.

One sentence, four ways
I want you with me always, my love
a man to a man'ayzak ma'aaya 'ala tool ya habeebi
a man to a woman'ayzik ma'aaya 'ala tool ya habibti
a woman to a man'ayzaak ma'aaya 'ala tool ya habeebi
a woman to a woman'ayzaaki ma'aaya 'ala tool ya habibti

The aa of 'ayzaak and 'ayzaaki means a woman is speaking. The -i of 'ayzik and 'ayzaaki, and habibti, mean a woman is listening. ma'aaya, with me, never changes.

Pseudo-verbs

Have, I'd love to, must. English says these with verbs. Egyptian uses a word with the endings from Possessives, where the ending says who, or a word that never changes.

'and- عند · have
I'andiwe'andina

It means at: 'andi 'arabiyya, I have a car. Not: ma-'andeesh, I don't have; ma-'andaksh, you don't have. Past: kaan 'andi, I had. Will: haykoon 'andi, I'll have.

ma'aak filoos? do you have money on you? ma-ma'eesh, I don't have any on me.

nifs- نفس · I'd love to
nifsi arooh masrI'd love to go to Egypt

Literally my soul: the warm, wishful cousin of 'aayiz. It takes the same endings, nifsi, nifsak, nifsik, nifsu, nifsaha, nifsina, and the verb after it is bare, as after 'aayiz.
